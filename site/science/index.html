The science

How Lactic works it out

Lactic's race times, pacing, fueling and training come from published models and research, not guesses. This page explains what each number is based on, what we chose where the research gives a range, and how far to trust it.

How far to trust it

The physics of riding a bike is well understood. With a rider's real drag and rolling resistance, a power model predicted measured road power with an error of 2.7 W (R² 0.97).1 What makes a prediction miss is usually the inputs, not the physics: your drag area, your tyres and the road, your form on the day, the wind, and who you ride with.

So the pre-race report gives a most likely time and a likely range. The range comes from re-running the model with each uncertain input at the slow and the fast end:

  • Drag area ±8% while Lactic uses a typical value for your kind of bike, or ±4% once it has measured yours.
  • Rolling resistance ±15%.
  • Your power on the day ±3%.
  • The draft ±8 points in a small group and ±15 in a bunch.

Climbs are the most reliable part. On a climb, gravity does most of the work, and gravity depends on weight and power, which you know well. On the flat, drag dominates and the inputs matter more. In a road race the bunch decides the speed, so a finish time there is a rough guide. What getting dropped on a climb costs you is worth more than the minute.

Speed and time

For every 100 m of the course, Lactic finds the speed at which the power you'll ride at balances what holds you back:

  • Gravity: from the grade and the weight of you and your bike.
  • Rolling resistance: from the tyres and the surface.
  • Air: from your drag area, your speed plus the wind, and the air's density, which falls with altitude.
  • Drivetrain losses: about 2.4%.

This is the model validated by Martin and colleagues.1 Typical values for each kind of event:

Measuring your own drag

Drag area is the input that varies most between riders. Position, size and clothing can move it by a third. So Lactic can measure yours from your recent outdoor rides with power, using their files from intervals.icu.

Each ride is cut into 30-second blocks. In each block, the energy you put through the pedals has to equal the energy that went into climbing, speeding up, rolling resistance and the air. With rolling resistance at a typical value, every block gives one estimate of your drag. This is the energy-balance form of Chung's "virtual elevation" method.19

  • Which blocks count: steady pedaling only. Descents, corners, stops and steep ramps are left out, because brakes and slow speeds make the estimate unreliable.
  • Per ride: the median over the blocks, which shrugs off gusts and GPS jitter.
  • Across rides: group rides read low because of the draft, so Lactic takes the median of your higher-reading rides.

Lactic then checks itself. It times each ride from that ride's own power, using the drag measured from your other rides, and tells you how close it came.

Riding in a group

Behind a single wheel, the following rider's drag drops by about 25% to 50%, depending on the gap and position.2,3 Deep inside a large peloton it drops by 90% or more.4 Real racing is messier: you move up, close gaps, take the wind on corners and in crosswinds, and spend time near the front.

Lactic takes 28% off your drag in a small group that shares turns, and half in a bunch, which sits well inside the published ranges. The draft fades below about 15 km/h, so it matters little on climbs. For group events, the report also times the course as if you were dropped at the foot of the main climb and rode the rest alone.

How hard to ride each part

Riding harder on climbs and into the wind, and easier on descents and with the wind behind you, is faster than holding even power for the same effort.5,6 Lactic paces each part of the course that way.

  • The whole race: an intensity factor (a share of FTP) for the kind of event and how hard it's raced. It eases as the event gets longer, following Allen and Coggan's ranges for each type of event.7
  • Climbs: the power-duration curve, scaled from FTP by how long the climb takes. That's about 120% of FTP for 3 minutes, 100% for about 20 minutes, and less for longer.7,8
  • Long days: hours of work lower what you can hold later on. This is called durability.9 Professionals rode a 20-minute time trial 2.9% lower after about 4 hours (from 8.5% lower to 1% higher).10 Amateurs tend to fade more, so Lactic takes 3% off climbs in events of 1.5 to 3 hours, 5% off up to 5 hours, and 8% off beyond that.

Altitude

Thinner air lowers drag, which helps, but it also lowers the oxygen you can use, which costs power. Lactic uses Bassett and colleagues' equations, built from cycling hour records, for aerobic power at altitude.11 There are separate curves for riders who live at altitude and for riders who've been there only a few days. A rider from near sea level holds about 12% less power at 2,000 m and 16% less at 2,500 m.

Your FTP was set where you live, so Lactic works the cost out from your home elevation, which it asks for when a course goes high. Racing below where you live costs nothing.

Heat

Trained cyclists rode a 30-minute time trial 6.5% lower at 32°C than at 23°C.12 Lactic takes about 0.7% off for each degree above 21°C, at most 15%, using the forecast temperature at each point on the course when you'll be there. Pacing targets in the report already include it.

Wind and weather

Forecasts come from Open-Meteo for up to five points along the course, from about 15 days out. They're refreshed every few hours, and more often in the last two days.

Forecast wind is given at 10 m above the ground. Near the ground it is slower: from the log wind profile, wind at a rider's height over open country is about 0.6 of the 10 m wind (less among trees and buildings).13 Lactic uses 0.6, resolves it along your direction of travel on each stretch, and feeds it into the speed model.

Fuel and fluid

  • During the race: none under 75 minutes (a gel before the start covers it), 60 g an hour for most events, and 90 g an hour for long hard ones. That's within 30 to 60 g for 1 to 2.5 hours and up to 90 g of mixed sugars beyond that.14,15
  • Carbohydrate loading: 10 g per kg a day for the two days before events over 90 minutes, the low end of 10 to 12 g/kg for 36 to 48 hours.14
  • Pre-race meal: 2 g/kg three hours before the start, within 1 to 4 g/kg 1 to 4 hours before.14
  • Fluid: 400 to 800 ml an hour, more as it gets warmer, to keep weight loss under 2%.16 Sweat rates vary a lot between people. Weighing yourself before and after a hot training ride tells you yours.
  • Sodium: about 575 mg in every liter you drink, the middle of 20 to 30 mmol per liter (460 to 690 mg).16

Feeds go every 20 minutes. They move to just before a climb, or over its top, so you aren't eating halfway up.

Target times

Set a finish time and Lactic runs the speed model backwards to find the FTP that rides the course in that time at the event's effort. It then compares that with a realistic FTP by race day.

A realistic gain is about 3% in a month of structured training, 6% in two months and 10% in five, slowing as it goes. Trained cyclists gained about 8% in 40-minute time-trial power over 12 weeks of structured training, with wide variation between riders.17 Newer riders often gain more and very experienced ones less.

Training for a course

Training works best when it looks like the event. When your A race has a course, Lactic shapes the plan around it:

  • Long climbs: sustained threshold and sweet-spot work in blocks as long as the main climb.
  • Short, steep kicks: repeated VO2 max efforts.
  • Flat courses: the plan trains for the kind of event.
  • A target time above your current FTP: base weeks swap tempo for threshold.

The last week before the event cuts volume roughly in half and keeps the intensity, in line with a meta-analysis of tapering (volume down 41% to 60%, intensity kept).18 Workouts are built by rules, not generated by AI. The AI coach, if you use one, reads these numbers. It never makes them up.
